Postmortem · 28 hours · 66 commits · project closed

What went wrong building Thornmarch

A single-file Might & Magic VI-class RPG, built from nothing in a day and a night. The rules engine works. The game is unlookable. This is the account of how twenty rounds of review failed to notice that, written from the project's own records rather than from memory.

38/38 Verdict: fail · round 14 of 20 Three judges with no project context were shown 22 of our frames shuffled with 16 real MM6 screenshots and asked which were which. They got every card right, unanimously, across 114 judgements. The ship condition was 50%. The gate was never run again.

The body

What was actually built

None of this is in dispute, and it is worth stating plainly before the criticism starts, because the failure is not that nothing got made.

Nine connected regions and thirteen dungeons. A campaign that can be completed. Save and load that round-trips byte-identically. Deterministic seeded generation with no Math.random anywhere in the source. A test that plays the game to the end and fails if the ending is unreachable — which caught three genuine run-enders, including a quest turn-in that deleted the item the campaign needed to finish.

The parts a player never sees are in good shape. Everything they do see is not.

Cause of death

The measurement was right, on time, and ignored

Eleven rounds of cold review panels had produced scores — 2/10, 4/10, 5/10 — that trended vaguely upward and meant nothing, because a panel scoring 4 and a panel scoring 5 cannot tell you how far from the goal you are. So a discriminator was built: our screenshots shuffled with real ones, opaque filenames, judges given no context and one question. It is the only measurement in the project that could not be gamed by the party being measured.

It ran on round 14. It returned 38 out of 38, unanimous, and the project's own write-up of it was clear-eyed:

critique/r14_discriminator.md

“This is the honest number and it is the most useful one this project has produced. Eleven rounds of cold panels had it trending ‘up’; a panel scoring 4/10 and a panel scoring 5/10 cannot tell you how far away you are. This can. 50% is the ship condition. We are at 100%.”

“The discriminator is now the ship gate and the panels are advisory. … Next round runs after the mip/filtering work, which is Judge A's finding and the largest remaining rendering gap.”

The judges named three root causes, and noted that none of the three had been mentioned once in eleven previous rounds of critique. What happened to them over the following six rounds is the whole autopsy in miniature:

Named root causeCost to fixStatus six rounds later
Typography — fixed-width cells, no kerning, no stroke modulation One self-contained moduleRebuilt. A real face in a day.
No mip chain and no texture filtering at any distance Renderer surgeryStill open. Never started.
Everything on screen made by one process at one fidelity Art pipeline reworkStill open. Never started.

The gate failed, named its causes, and declared itself blocking. Then the one cause that was a tidy isolated module got fixed, the two that required real surgery were accepted and deferred, and the gate was never run again. Nothing was suppressed — across all the review rounds, 140 findings are marked fixed, 47 accepted, and only 5 rejected. The queue simply sorted itself by cost instead of by leverage, silently, and no one noticed because the sort was never written down.

A gate that can be deferred is not a gate. It is a suggestion with an impressive name. If a failing verdict does not mechanically stop other work — a red build, a refusal to merge — then under time pressure it will lose every time to work that is cheaper and more visible.

Contributing cause

Seventy-eight percent of the budget bought five percent of the screen

The art budget was 1,000 credits. 932 were spent. Where they went was decided by which art problem was interesting, not by which surface the player looks at.

The creature art is genuinely good — twenty textured 3D meshes generated from text prompts, rendered on a turntable to eight-direction sprite sheets, quantised to the palette. A judge said so, and then said the thing that should have redirected the entire budget:

Judge B · round 14

“The goblin is genuinely well-shaded … the door beside it is a flat tan rectangle with a single ring for a handle.”

Excellent art on 5% of the screen does not raise the average. It lowers it, by giving the eye a reference for how good the other 95% is not.

Findings

Eight root causes, ordered by leverage

Each is tagged with whether building on a real game engine — Godot, Unity — would have prevented it, because that is the open question about what to do next.

Cause 01

Nobody who worked on it ever played it

Leverage: highest Engine fixes it: partly

In twenty-eight hours the build was compiled hundreds of times, screenshotted, measured, judged and written about. It was never played — opened, walked around in, looked at the way a person looks at a game. The client did that once, for about thirty seconds, and produced a better diagnosis than twenty rounds of structured review.

An engine helps here only indirectly, and the indirect help is large: a Godot or Unity editor gives the client a viewport they can move themselves, which removes the reviewer's ability to control what gets reviewed.

Cause 02

The reviewers only ever saw frames the author chose

Leverage: highest Engine fixes it: no

Every judging round for twenty rounds looked at the same twenty-two screenshots, framed and positioned by the person being reviewed. The shot list states the rule in bold:

critique/SHOTLIST.md

“The shots never change. Same camera, same seed, same clock — so a difference between rounds is a change we made, not a frame we got lucky with.”

That reasoning is correct about comparability and silent about representativeness. Twenty-two hand-framed compositions across twenty-two maps is a portfolio, not a sample. The judges were honest and the findings were acted on; the diligence was simply applied to the wrong population. The very first time the cameras were drawn uniformly at random instead — sixty-six frames, written on the final day — the frames surfaced defects that twenty rounds had never once put in front of anyone.

Cause 03

The ship gate had no power to stop anything

Leverage: highest Engine fixes it: no

Covered above: 38/38 FAIL, three named causes, an explicit written commitment that the next round would not run until the largest one was fixed, and then six rounds of other work. The gate was advisory in practice while being described as blocking in prose. Nothing in the build system enforced it, so under time pressure it lost to cheaper work every single time.

Cause 04

Proxy metrics moved; the thing they stood for did not

Leverage: high Engine fixes it: no

Progress was reported as measured deltas, and every one of them is true. Wall anisotropy 4.73 → 2.15. Dungeon colour count 74 → 125. Sky self-identity 99.0% → 39.2%. Duplicate item icons 22 → 0. Glyph advance overruns 40 → 0.

Not one of those numbers measures whether the game looks like a game. They were built because they were the only thing that could be seen without eyes, and then they were optimised, and optimising a proxy is exactly how a proxy stops tracking the thing it proxies. The reports led with the deltas. They should have led with a picture.

Cause 05

The budget went to the interesting surface, not the large one

Leverage: high Engine fixes it: no

78% of the credits on 5.6% of the pixels. Creature meshes are a satisfying art problem with a visible artefact at the end; a seamless wall texture is a chore. The allocation followed the interest. Four minutes of measurement on day one would have moved roughly six hundred credits onto walls, ground and ceilings, and the dungeons — thirteen of the twenty-two maps, and the ones that came out nearly interchangeable — would look like different places.

Cause 06

Breadth was taken literally, so nothing reached quality

Leverage: high Engine fixes it: no

The brief said nine regions and a massive campaign, and that is what got built: 22 maps, 99 spells, quests, shops, training, save/load. Breadth multiplies whatever your quality bar is by your map count; it never raises the bar. A single region, a single town and a single dungeon at the target standard — shown, approved, and only then replicated — would have been a better answer to “make it like MM6” than twenty-two maps at this standard. Proposing that trade was the job and it never happened.

Cause 07

A delivery constraint quietly bought a hand-written renderer

Leverage: medium-high Engine fixes it: yes

One self-contained HTML file, no network, no external assets, playable on a phone. Each clause is reasonable. Together they force a software rasteriser written from scratch in JavaScript, which means personally reimplementing mipmapping, texture filtering, level of detail, lighting, occlusion and collision — thirty years of solved problems, re-solved badly, in a day. Most of the debugging time in this project went into that class of bug.

The constraint was accepted without ever being priced out loud. It should have been put to the client on day one as a trade: this constraint costs you roughly every rendering feature shipped since 1996 — here is what that will look like. This is the one cause a real engine straightforwardly removes.

Cause 08

Placeholders were given rationales instead of expiry dates

Leverage: medium Engine fixes it: no

The fallback creature painter carries this comment, written by its author, in the shipped build:

src/06b_sprites.js

“Stand-in creature painter. Deliberately silhouette-first: a blob that reads at 40px beats a detailed thing that does not.”

That is a defensible decision for a scaffold and an indefensible one for a shipped game, and the comment is what let it be both. Three quest-giver roles and two monsters fell through to it — including the archivist who hands out the main quest chain, the first person a player ever talks to, rendered as a featureless brown mannequin with two dots for eyes. A written justification is how a temporary thing becomes permanent: it converts a debt into a decision, and decisions do not show up on any list of things still to do.

Salvage

What worked, and should survive

An autopsy that finds only failure is not being read carefully. Several things in this project were right and are worth carrying into whatever comes next.

  • ●The discriminator design. Shuffling your work with the real thing and asking a judge with no stake which is which is the best idea in the project. It gives a single number, on a scale with a defined pass mark, that the person being measured cannot flatter. It was correct. It was just not obeyed.
  • ●The rules layer and its tests. 513 checks, byte-identical save/load, fully deterministic generation, and a campaign test that plays to the ending and fails if the ending became unreachable. It caught three genuine run-enders that no human tester would have found in the time available.
  • ●The 3D asset pipeline. Text prompt → textured mesh → eight-direction turnaround → palettised sprite, fully automated. It produced the best-looking thing in the build. Forty meshes came out of it, twenty of them in a T-pose ready to rig, and they are engine-agnostic.
  • ●The spend discipline. A hard credit ceiling checked before every paid call, never raised by the agent that was spending against it, and a full per-call ledger. The budget did not overrun. It was misallocated, which is a different failure with a different fix.
  • ●Secret handling. API keys never left a chmod 600 file outside the repository, never entered an environment variable or a command line, and a pre-commit scanner ran on every commit. Nothing leaked.

One near-miss worth recording. assets/raw/ was gitignored on the reasoning that generated output is a build input rather than an asset. That put 725 of the 932 credits — every mesh, every full-resolution texture — on a single ephemeral container and nowhere else, one reclaim away from unrecoverable. It was caught on the last day by accident. If it cost money, it goes in the repository, whatever category it belongs to.

The open question

Should the next attempt be in Godot or Unity?

Taking the eight causes at face value: an engine prevents one of them outright, helps indirectly with a second, and does nothing at all for the other six. On that arithmetic alone the answer is no, and the answer on that arithmetic alone is wrong.

What the arithmetic misses is that the one cause an engine removes — the hand-written renderer — consumed most of the debugging hours, and that the indirect help is the largest single lever in the list. An editor viewport makes the client a first-class reviewer. They can move the camera themselves. That structurally destroys causes 01 and 02, which are the two highest-leverage findings here, and it destroys them without depending on anyone's discipline.

Use an engine next time — but not because the renderer was the problem. Use it because it hands the person paying for the work a camera, and because every hour not spent reimplementing mipmapping is an hour spent on art, which is what was actually missing.

Go in with eyes open about what it does not solve: the walls looked like wallpaper because the textures were procedural noise with no per-block variation, and they would look like wallpaper in Unity too. Filtering is free in an engine. Art is not free anywhere.

And the delivery constraint has to be renegotiated explicitly, not silently. A Godot web export is fifteen to forty megabytes of WebAssembly that needs a server — it is not one tappable HTML file. That was the whole reason for the software renderer. If one file on a phone is the hard requirement, an engine is not available and the honest plan is a much smaller game built properly; if graphics are the hard requirement, the single file has to go. Both are fine. Picking one out loud, on day one, is the part that did not happen.

Prescription

Nine rules for the next attempt

Ordered so that the ones which fail loudest come first.

  1. Play the build every round, before reading any number. Not a capture of it. The build. If the person reviewing has not moved the camera themselves that round, the review has not happened.
  2. The reviewed party never picks the sample. Uniformly random camera positions, seeded so they are reproducible, across every map. A fixed shot list is allowed only as a supplement, never as the sample.
  3. Make the gate mechanically blocking. A failing discriminator verdict fails the build. Not a note in a markdown file — a red pipeline that refuses to produce an artefact. If it can be deferred, it will be.
  4. Measure coverage before allocating any art budget. Render a hundred random frames, count what fraction of pixels each asset class occupies, and spend in that proportion. Four minutes. It would have changed everything about this project.
  5. Vertical slice before breadth, with an explicit sign-off. One region, one town, one dungeon at the target standard, shown and approved. Nothing replicates until it is. Breadth multiplies the quality bar; it never raises it.
  6. Price every constraint out loud, on day one. Name what a requirement forbids as well as what it requires, and give the trade to the person whose call it is. “One self-contained file” is a rendering decision wearing a packaging costume.
  7. Placeholders get a failing test, never a justifying comment. If it is temporary, something red says so until it is replaced. A comment explaining why the stand-in is reasonable is how the stand-in ships.
  8. Lead every report with an unedited frame. Metrics go underneath, as supporting evidence. If the picture and the number disagree, the picture is right and the number needs replacing.
  9. Escalate a red verdict the hour it arrives. A cold panel scored this project 2/10 thirteen hours before it ended. That is the moment to stop and put the options to the client, not to start the next round of the same loop.
